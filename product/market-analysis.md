Product · Market analysis · October 2026

# Win pilots at the career-transition moment

There is a real opening for a small, profitable logbook business in October 2026, but most of your proposed differentiators don't create it. The incumbents are a bundled EFB logbook (ForeFlight, Garmin), an Apple-only premium product (LogTen), a free but dated hobbyist favorite (MyFlightbook), and a group of desktop-era apps with weak support (Safelog, Logbook Pro, CrewLounge, FlyLog). Across all of them, pilots' worst complaints concern trust, not looks: sync failures that lose hours, support that goes unanswered for weeks, and prices that pile up across add-ons. **Three of your ten features open a real path. Automated paper-logbook digitization with a pre-interview audit (7) is the strongest. A CFI correct-and-sign workflow that meets the new REED rules (10) comes next. First-class Android and web, priced below LogTen (1), is the third.** Reports, totals, currency, reminders and a free student tier (5, 6, 8, 9) are table stakes that you must build but that won't win anyone over. Friend-following and a family live map (2, 4) are the riskiest items: crowded, free elsewhere, and exposed to crew-security and LADD problems. The best target is career-track pilots between CFI time-building and their first years at a regional airline, especially those not on Apple devices. Enter as an "interview-ready logbook," priced at about $60–$80 a year plus a one-time digitization fee. At that price, **roughly 900–1,800 paying subscribers produce $5k–$10k in net monthly revenue**. That is under half a percent of the U.S. certificated-pilot base. The market is big enough, so the question is whether you can win pilots' trust and pull them away from tools they already use.

## A growing pilot base, consolidating incumbents, and no modern cross-platform leader

Demand is growing. The FAA counted **887,519 active U.S. pilots at year-end 2025, up 4.6% in a year and up from 691,691 in 2020**. That includes 118,314 commercial pilots (+7.8%) and 181,742 ATPs ([AVweb](https://avweb.com/aviation-news/faa-pilot-population-climbs-again-in-2025/)). There are also 145,538 active flight instructors, who overlap with those commercial and ATP counts ([AFM.aero](https://afm.aero/us-flight-training-sector-data-report-reveals-record-student-growth-rising-commercial-conversions-and-expanding-women-pilot-pipeline)). The 370,286 student count overstates active training, because student certificates issued since 2016 never expire ([AOPA](https://www.aopa.org/news-and-media/all-news/2026/august/25/the-pipeline-squeeze)). A better measure is new student certificates, which run at about **59,000 a year and have been falling since 2023** ([AFM.aero](https://afm.aero/us-flight-training-sector-data-report-reveals-record-student-growth-rising-commercial-conversions-and-expanding-women-pilot-pipeline)). Airline hiring has swung sharply. It fell from about 13,000 hires in 2022 to about 4,500 in 2025, and 2026 is a recovery year ([ALPA](https://www.alpa.org/articles/2026/10/pilot-employment-trends-outlook-remains-strong)). Regionals now prefer 50–100 hours of multi-engine time and "can raise standards" because applicants compete harder ([AOPA](https://www.aopa.org/news-and-media/all-news/2026/august/25/the-pipeline-squeeze)). That raises the value of an accurate, auditable record of hours.

The incumbents are consolidating into larger platforms. **Boeing sold ForeFlight and Jeppesen to Thoma Bravo for $10.55B, and the private-equity-owned company relaunched in November 2025** ([AVweb](https://avweb.com/aviation-news/jeppesen-foreflight-under-private-equity/)). A price increase followed in February 2026, taking Starter from $125 to $130 a year ([PriceTimeline](https://pricetimeline.com/news/206)). ForeFlight's logbook is sold only inside its EFB plans, and the mobile app is iOS-only ([ForeFlight support](https://support.foreflight.com/hc/en-us/articles/215562477-How-do-I-add-Logbook-to-my-subscription); [ForeFlight support](https://support.foreflight.com/hc/en-us/articles/115004919307-Does-ForeFlight-work-on-Android-devices-)). **LogTen**, the leading paid standalone logbook (4.7 stars from about 9,900 ratings), belongs to flight-school scheduling company Flight Schedule Pro. It was rebranded "LogTen by Pilotbase" in February 2026 and is still Apple-only ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/app/id837274884); [LogTen support](https://support.logten.com/en/articles/13836844-what-s-pilotbase)). Garmin Pilot is the only big platform with an Android app, and that app is rated **about 2.6 stars, against 4.7 on iOS** ([AppPricingLab](https://apppricinglab.com/app/google_play/com.digcy.pilot)). MyFlightbook is free, open source and on all three platforms. In one recent week, 5,600 pilots logged 21,300 flights on it ([MyFlightbook](https://myflightbook.com/logbook/mvc/pub/about)). One analytics aggregator describes its development as "maintenance-level" ([marlvel.ai](https://marlvel.ai/apps/myflightbook)).

The 2024–2026 newcomers (Skyden, PilotLogX, Logger, LogbookAI, TopLog) are almost all Apple-only indie apps priced at about $30–$80 a year. Several offer AI scanning of paper logbooks. **None has enough U.S. App Store ratings to show a score** ([Skyden](https://apps.apple.com/app/skyden/id6745750700); [Logger](https://apps.apple.com/lb/app/pilot-logbook-logger/id6470538989); [TopLog](https://apps.apple.com/app/id6756334477)). So competition for attention is getting crowded, but no newcomer has won it. And no current product combines all of the following: modern design, iOS plus Android plus web, reliable airline import, a good support reputation and a fair price.

Dated UI is nearly universal, but it is rarely the reason someone leaves. A pilot who loses hours right before a job interview has a career problem, not a usability problem. That is why trust in data integrity drives purchases more than any feature does.

## Target career-track pilots with an "interview-ready logbook" wedge

**Recommended segment:** U.S. career-track pilots, starting with CFIs and time-builders and continuing to regional first officers in their first five years, with extra emphasis on Android users. This group pays and is underserved. Commercial certificates grew 7.8% in 2025. Professionals already pay $100–$180 a year for schedule import (LogTen Pro at $129.99; Logbook Pro plus Schedule Importer at about $180). The hiring market rewards precisely documented multi, turbine and PIC time ([AOPA](https://www.aopa.org/news-and-media/all-news/2026/august/25/the-pipeline-squeeze); [App Store, Logbook Pro](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/logbook-pro-flight-logbook/id410773111)). Avoid GA hobbyists as a primary target. They are attached to free MyFlightbook and to EFB bundles they already pay for, and one forum thread called MyFlightbook "unanimous" ([PilotsOfAmerica](https://www.pilotsofamerica.com/community/threads/on-line-log-book-program-suggestions.66234/post-1326777)). Avoid EASA at launch: acceptance is decided state by state, and Swiss FOCA requires a formal paid application ([FOCA](https://www.bazl.admin.ch/dam/bazl/en/dokumente/Personal/gm-info_personal/foca_gminfo_foca-acccepted-logbook_formats.pdf.download.pdf/GMINFO%20FOCA%20Accepted%20Logbook%20Formats%2020240201.pdf)).

**Wedge strategy:** Sell "audit-ready before your interview." Step one: a pilot photographs their paper logbook. The AI drafts entries, the pilot verifies them against the source-page images, and the audit engine flags errors. Examples include column sums that don't match page totals, carry-forward mistakes, dual entries missing CFI signatures or certificate data, cross-country entries that don't meet the definition, and gaps in dates. Step two: the clean logbook becomes their ongoing subscription logbook. Step three: one tap produces the interview packet. Position it as "AI-drafted, pilot-verified." TopLog already works that way, and OCR errors on a legal record are a liability ([App Store: TopLog](https://apps.apple.com/app/id6756334477)). Pair this with flawless importers for ForeFlight, LogTen, MyFlightbook and Garmin CSVs that **recalculate and show currency** after import. Garmin's silent zero-approaches failure shows how damaging this step is when it goes wrong. Offer a written guarantee that export always works, even after a subscription lapses. Pilot Partner already markets that ([Dronedesk](https://blog.dronedesk.io/pilot-logbook-apps/)). For CFIs, the hook is a correct-and-sign workflow, a PDF of endorsements given, and REED fields ahead of the Phase 2 deadline on **March 1, 2027**. For distribution, copy the paths that worked for LogTen: flight-school bulk licenses, pilot-association trials such as NGPA, a booth at AirVenture, and aviation podcasts ([Coradine](https://coradine.com/logten-pro-trial-for-ngpa/); [Alex MacPhail Podcast](https://alexmacphailpodcast.buzzsprout.com/1477786/episodes/13335628-133-noah-lieberman-founder-logten-pro)). Airline schedule import is the main professional upsell. Build it in stages using file, ICS and share-sheet import rather than stored airline credentials. Expect it to take the longest to build, because every airline format is custom and APDL claims more than 200 of them ([App Store: APDL](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/id669185248)).

**Pricing:** Make the logbook free through the private pilot certificate, matching LogTen. Offer a discounted CFI tier to win the time-building years. Price Pro at **$69–$79 a year with schedule import included**, which undercuts LogTen Pro ($129.99) and the stacked Logbook Pro add-ons. Sell digitization and audit as a one-time purchase in roughly the **$99–$299** range, scaled to entry count. That sits under the AcuLog and Fiverr human-service anchors. Bill on the web through Stripe where you can. Apple's Small Business Program and Google subscriptions take 15%, and web billing cuts the number of subscribers you need by about 12% ([RevenueCat](https://www.revenuecat.com/blog/engineering/small-business-program)). Safelog already proves a web-direct channel works ([Dauntless](https://www.dauntless-soft.com/HelpDesk/phraseview.asp?PID=561)). Don't sell lifetime licenses that you can't honor. CrewLounge's conversion of "lifetime" licenses still draws "scam" and "abusive" accusations years later ([AirlinePilotForums](https://www.airlinepilotforums.com/2817143-post5.html); [Trustpilot](https://at.trustpilot.com/review/crewlounge.aero?page=10)). Avoid a generous freemium model too. At typical 2–5% freemium conversion (a general SaaS assumption, not a figure measured for aviation apps), you would need more than 100,000 free users. A usage-gated trial, like LogTen's free-until-50-hours, converts better.

## Signatures, crew privacy, LADD and sync bugs are the risks that can sink you

**FAA e-signatures (AC 120-78B).** The FAA issued AC 120-78B on December 11, 2024, replacing 120-78A. It is "an acceptable means, but not the only means," and a draft Change 1 drew industry comments in February 2026 ([FAA](https://www.faa.gov/regulations_policies/advisory_circulars/index.cfm/go/document.information/documentID/1043396); [ARSA/AEA](https://arsa.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/ARSA-AEA-FAA-DRAFTAC_120_78B-Comments-02262026.pdf)). For Part 61 and Part 91 records, **no FAA approval exists to obtain or advertise**, but the FAA "may question" records that don't meet the AC's attributes. Those attributes include uniqueness, intent, sole control, non-repudiation, traceability and permanence ([AC 120-78A](https://www.faa.gov/documentLibrary/media/Advisory_Circular/AC_120-78A.pdf)). Don't market anything as "FAA-approved." Instead, follow MyFlightbook and publish an attribute-by-attribute compliance mapping. Use tamper-evident hashing, make any edit invalidate the signature while keeping earlier versions, and keep signed endorsements available offline. The offline requirement exists because students on solo cross-countries and sport pilots must carry their endorsements ([MyFlightbook](https://myflightbook.com/logbook/mvc/pub/CFISigs); [eCFR 61.51](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/chapter-I/subchapter-D/part-61/subpart-A/section-61.51)). Endorsements need the instructor's certificate number plus the expiration date or REED, and REED alone from March 1, 2027. Instructors must keep endorsement records for three years, so CFI-side records have to survive even if a student deletes their account ([eCFR 61.189](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/chapter-I/subchapter-H/part-61/subpart-H/section-61.189)). **Check-airman sign-offs fall under Part 121 and Part 135 programs that need the operator's OpSpec A025 authorization.** You can't authorize that yourself, so feature 10's check-airman half is a slow B2B sale to operators, not something you ship as an app feature ([AC 120-78A](https://www.faa.gov/documentLibrary/media/Advisory_Circular/AC_120-78A.pdf)). Signature portability is also a barrier to switching. Signatures made in ForeFlight or MyFlightbook can't be re-verified cryptographically in your app, so import them as images with provenance.

**Crew location privacy.** In June 2025 British Airways banned crew from posting layover-hotel photos, even on private accounts, "to eliminate all risk" of identifying where crews stay. The ban came weeks after a flight attendant died at an SFO layover hotel ([One Mile at a Time](https://onemileatatime.com/news/british-airways-bans-crew-social-media-posts-layover-hotels/)). United moved in 2020 to eliminate third-party scheduling apps ([United AFA](https://unitedafa.org/news/united-doubles-down-on-third-party-application-policy)), and one Flight Crew View reviewer said their employer treated the app's login as a security risk ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/app/id999316238)). A breach exposing future crew itineraries would end your company. If you build feature 2, make it opt-in and city-level only, never show hotels, and limit sharing by time. Copy CrewConnect's per-audience visibility controls. Avoid storing airline credentials. The same United precedent means any schedule importer built on credential scraping can be shut off overnight.

**LADD and PIA for the live map.** If you show GA flights using FAA-derived data, you must hide aircraft on the LADD list, show none of their history, and apply the monthly list updates. Vendors that violate these rules can lose FAA data access. Showing an aircraft that uses a Privacy ICAO Address by its tail number defeats the purpose of the program ([NBAA](https://nbaa.org/aircraft-operations/security/privacy/limiting-aircraft-data-displayed-ladd/)). The safe design shares the pilot's own phone GPS with invited viewers through expiring links. That information is the pilot's choice to disclose, not FAA data.

**Business risks.** First, sync reliability. It is the top complaint in this category, and a single data-loss bug from a solo developer would get you the same reviews that sank CrewLounge and FlyLog. Second, the "bus factor" criticism leveled at MyFlightbook will apply to you too. Answer it with guaranteed export and a published data-continuity promise. Third, the digitization gap is closing. TopLog, LogbookAI, PilotLogX, LogbookScan and others launched between late 2025 and 2026, so your edge has to be audit quality and verification, not OCR alone. Fourth, professional demand moves with airline hiring, which fell about 60% in 2024 ([AirlineGeeks](https://airlinegeeks.com/2025/01/16/pilot-hiring-nosedives-in-2024/)). CFIs and time-builders keep logging through downturns, which is one more reason to start with them. Fifth, ForeFlight or LogTen could close the printing, CFI-editing or iOS-import gaps. LogTen's iOS schedule import is already in beta.

These figures leave out VAT, which adds about 5–15% for international users. They also leave out your operating costs (hosting, OCR inference, support tools, insurance), which the research didn't quantify. For a solo founder, "modest profit" probably means **$60k–$120k a year, or about 900–1,800 subscribers at $80**, or fewer if most billing goes through Stripe. Digitization revenue comes on top. As an illustration, not a forecast: 300 one-time audits a year at $150 would add about $45k. Compared with roughly 474,000 certificated private, commercial and ATP pilots, 1,800 subscribers is under 0.4%. LogTen shows the ceiling is much higher. Its own claim of 80,000 to 160,000 pilots, even at a heavily discounted paying share, implies a business with several million dollars in annual revenue, big enough to attract a strategic buyer ([Flight Schedule Pro](https://www.flightschedulepro.com/blog/coradine-acquired-by-flight-schedule-pro); [App Store](https://apps.apple.com/app/id837274884)). The research found no pilot-app-specific conversion or churn data. Validate the economics early by pre-selling digitization audits to CFIs and regional applicants before building the full logbook.

## Conclusion

The opportunity is different from what your feature list assumes. You don't win this market by being a nicer logbook with social extras. You win by becoming the trustworthy custodian of a legal record at the moments pilots are willing to move it: when they digitize paper history, when they start instructing, and when they apply to an airline. In that framing, the audit engine is more than a feature. It turns a one-time migration expense into the start of a subscription, and it gives you a reason to reach pilots years before LogTen does. A free-tier student who is still with you at their regional interview is worth more than any number of followers on a social map.

Treat the consolidation of ForeFlight into a private-equity roll-up and LogTen into a scheduling SaaS as an opening. Pilots worried about lock-in now have a reason to want a vendor-neutral record that outlives any one EFB subscription. Guaranteed export, a published AC 120-78B compliance mapping and REED-ready endorsements become positioning in their own right, not just compliance work. The most likely good outcome for a small, trusted logbook is the one the category has already produced several times: steady modest profit, and eventually an acquisition by a larger platform, as happened with LogTen, CloudAhoy and BlackBox.
